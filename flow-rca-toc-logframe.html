Design chain · RCA → ToC → Logframe → MEAL products

What a programme measures is decided long before anyone collects anything.

This is the chain that turns a community problem into a measurable programme. Get it wrong and every dashboard downstream reports the wrong thing accurately.

Runs atProgramme design & redesign
Facilitated withCommunity, partners, technical specialists, government
Typical duration4–8 weeks
Ends withAn operational MEAL pack
STEP 01
↓
Situation analysis

Before any tree is drawn, establish what is actually happening and to whom. Without this, the root cause analysis becomes a room full of opinions.

What is needed
  • Secondary data: national and district statistics, sector assessments, health and education records
  • Existing evaluations and lessons learned from previous programme cycles
  • Community consultation, including children where the programme concerns them
  • Stakeholder mapping — who holds power, who delivers services, who is excluded
  • A GESI lens applied from the start, not added later
Process
  • Define the geographic scope and the target population precisely
  • Assemble the evidence and identify who is most vulnerable and why
  • Validate findings with community members and local government before moving on
Output
  • A situation analysis narrative and a single, agreed problem statement
STEP 02
↓
Root Cause Analysis

A participatory problem tree: the core problem in the middle, its effects above, its causes below. The work is in going deep enough to reach causes a programme can actually act on.

What is needed
  • The validated problem statement from Step 01
  • A workshop with community representatives, partner staff, technical specialists and government counterparts
  • Facilitation materials and enough time — this cannot be rushed into a half-day
Process
  • Place the core problem, then build downwards by asking why until the answers stop moving
  • Separate causes into immediate, underlying and root — most programmes intervene at the underlying level
  • Test every branch against the evidence from Step 01; unsupported branches are assumptions, not causes
  • Mark which causes the programme can address, which require advocacy, and which are out of scope — and say so openly
Distinguishing a cause from a symptom
  • A cause, if removed, removes the effect. A symptom disappears when the cause is fixed, but fixing the symptom changes nothing underneath.
  • If you cannot act on it and cannot influence it, it is context — record it as an assumption or a risk instead of pretending it is a cause.
Output
  • A validated problem tree with an evidenced causal chain
STEP 03
↓
Problem tree into objective tree

The mechanical step that makes the rest possible: each negative condition is restated as the positive condition that would exist if it were solved.

Process
  • Restate every problem as an achieved positive condition, keeping the same structure
  • Causes become outputs and activities; effects become outcomes and impact
  • Check the means–ends logic upward: if all the lower boxes are achieved, is the box above genuinely achieved?
  • Prune the branches the programme has decided not to address, and record why
Output
  • An objective tree — the skeleton of the Theory of Change
STEP 04
↓
Theory of Change

The objective tree says what will change. The Theory of Change says why anyone should believe it will — and what has to be true for it to work.

What is needed
  • The objective tree from Step 03
  • An evidence base for each proposed causal link — research, previous evaluations, sector practice
  • A dedicated session to surface assumptions, which teams consistently leave implicit
Process
  • Define the impact first, then work backwards: outcomes, intermediate outcomes, outputs, activities, inputs
  • Write the assumption between each level explicitly — the condition that must hold for the lower level to produce the higher one
  • Attach the rationale or evidence for each link, so reviewers can see why the pathway is credible
  • Identify the risks that would break each pathway and where they would be detected
Output
  • A ToC diagram and narrative, with assumptions and evidence recorded against each level
STEP 05
↓
Logframe

Where the reasoning becomes a commitment. The ToC explains the logic; the logframe is what the programme will be held to.

What is needed
  • The completed ToC
  • The organisational standard indicator library, checked before writing anything custom
  • Donor indicator requirements, which rarely map one-to-one onto the ToC
Process
  • Convert each ToC level into a results statement: goal, outcome, output, activity
  • Select indicators — standard indicators first, custom only where nothing existing answers the question
  • Balance quantitative and qualitative: coverage numbers alone rarely evidence an outcome
  • For each indicator set baseline, target, means of verification, frequency and the person responsible
  • Carry the assumptions column across from the ToC rather than inventing new ones
  • Where donor indicators and ToC indicators diverge, map both and report to each — do not quietly drop one
Output
  • A logframe matrix that a donor, an auditor and a field team can all read
STEP 06
↓
MEAL products

A logframe is not yet a system. These are the documents that make it operational — and the ones auditors ask for first.

What gets produced
  • Indicator Reference Sheet — per indicator: definition, calculation, numerator and denominator, disaggregation, frequency, data source, collection method, responsible person, and the quality checks applied
  • MEL Plan — what will be monitored, evaluated and learned, by whom and when
  • Data Management Plan — storage, access rights, protection, retention, consent and safeguarding of personal data
  • RASCI matrix — who is responsible, accountable, supportive, consulted and informed at each step
  • Collection tools — KoboToolbox forms, Indicator Tracking Table, Monitoring Tracking Table
  • Baseline and evaluation plan — study design, sampling, timing and budget
  • Accountability mechanism — feedback and complaints channels, response standards, and how findings re-enter programming
  • Learning agenda — the questions the programme intends to answer, not just the boxes it intends to tick
Output
  • An operational MEAL pack a new staff member could pick up and run
STEP 07
Operationalise and test

The design is only proven once a figure has travelled the whole route and arrived correct at the other end.

Process
  • Pilot the collection form with real enumerators, not in a meeting room
  • Run dummy records end to end — form, storage, cleaning, tracking table, dashboard — and confirm the number that arrives is the number that was entered
  • Train enumerators and partner staff on the tools and the counting rules together, not separately
  • Verify the first live batch before it reaches any report
  • Only then build the dashboard on top
Output
  • A live monitoring system, with a tested path from field to figure
Where this chain usually breaks
  • RCA treated as a formality. The tree is drawn to satisfy a template, then the logframe is written from sector habit instead — so indicators measure activity, not the causes identified.
  • Assumptions left implicit. If nobody wrote down what has to be true, nobody notices when it stops being true.
  • Indicators chosen for collectability. Easy to count is not the same as answers the outcome question.
  • Reference sheets written after collection starts. Two teams count the same indicator differently and the discrepancy only appears at reconciliation, months later.
  • The ToC filed after approval. Treated as a proposal artefact rather than a live document, it stops reflecting how the programme actually works within a year.
Questions this process answers in an interview
  • Walk me through designing a MEAL system from scratch. The seven steps above, in order, with the emphasis on Step 02 — everything downstream inherits its quality.
  • What is the difference between a Theory of Change and a logframe? The ToC is the reasoning: why change is expected and what must hold true. The logframe is the contract: what will be measured, reported and answered for. One explains, the other commits.
  • How do you distinguish a root cause from a symptom? Remove it and see whether the effect goes with it. If you cannot act on it or influence it, it belongs in assumptions or risks, not in the tree.
  • How do you select indicators? Standard indicator library first, custom only where nothing fits; a balance of quantitative and qualitative; and every indicator traceable to a specific level of the ToC.
  • What if donor indicators do not match the ToC? Map both, report to both, and be explicit about the difference rather than silently reshaping the programme around the reporting form.
